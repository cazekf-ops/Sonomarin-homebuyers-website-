Selling a Fire-Damaged House in Sonoma County — What Homeowners Need to Know

By Evan & Alex, Sonomarin Home Buyers  |  Serving Sonoma & Marin County, CA

If you've been through a wildfire, you already know it's not a single event you recover from — it's a years-long decision tree. Sonoma County has lived through this more than most: the 2017 Tubbs Fire, the 2019 Kincade Fire, and the 2020 Glass Fire each left thousands of local homeowners staring at the same question — rebuild, or sell? This guide walks through the real math behind that decision, how your insurance claim interacts with a sale, and what to expect if you decide selling as-is is the right move.

You Don't Have to Wait for Your Claim to Settle

A lot of homeowners assume they need a fully resolved insurance claim before they can sell. That's not true. You can sell a fire-damaged property with an open claim, and in many cases the remaining claim proceeds can be assigned to the buyer or credited at closing, depending on your specific policy and where the claim currently stands. A buyer experienced with fire-damaged property will know how to structure this with your insurer rather than asking you to untangle it first.

The Real Rebuild-vs-Sell Math

Rebuilding after a total loss sounds straightforward on paper: take the insurance payout, hire a contractor, move back in. In practice, homeowners who rebuilt after the Tubbs and Glass fires commonly reported the process taking two to four years from permit application to move-in — sometimes longer for custom homes. Three things tend to blow up the timeline and the budget:

  • Construction costs rose faster than most payouts anticipated. Even a generous insurance settlement from several years ago often falls short of what it now costs to rebuild the same square footage.
  • Permitting backlogs. Both Sonoma County and the City of Santa Rosa saw a surge of rebuild permit applications after each fire, which meant real delays getting plans approved and inspections scheduled.
  • Contractor availability. A flood of rebuild projects hitting the market at once means good contractors book out far in advance, and some homeowners waited a year or more just to get a crew started.

None of this means rebuilding is the wrong choice — for many families it's the right one. But it's a multi-year project, not a repair, and it's worth being honest with yourself about whether you have the time, patience, and risk tolerance for it before you commit.

What a Fire-Damaged Property Is Actually Worth

Even when the structure is a total loss, the property isn't worth nothing. The land itself holds value, and depending on what survived, you may have more than just dirt to sell:

  • The lot. Location, size, and zoning drive most of the underlying value, fire or no fire.
  • Existing utility connections. A water meter, sewer lateral, or septic system already in place and permitted can save a future buyer significant time and money over starting from raw land.
  • A usable foundation. If the foundation survived and passes inspection, it can sometimes be built on again, which is a real cost savings for whoever rebuilds.
  • Standing structure, even if damaged. A partial loss with smoke or structural damage still has value as a renovation project for the right buyer.

The honest answer is that every fire-damaged property is different, and the only way to know what yours is worth is to have someone walk it who actually knows how to value this kind of property — not run it through an automated estimate built for move-in-ready homes.

Why Selling As-Is Often Makes Sense

If you don't want to take on a multi-year rebuild — whether because you've relocated, you're not up for the project, or the math just doesn't pencil out — selling as-is has real advantages:

  • No debris removal required. You don't need a debris removal permit or a hauling contract before you sell; a cash buyer can take the property as it sits.
  • No waiting on your claim. As covered above, an open claim doesn't have to hold up the sale.
  • No competing with financed buyers. Most lenders won't finance a fire-damaged or unbuildable lot, which narrows the buyer pool to cash buyers and investors anyway — so you're not giving up much by going straight to one.
  • You stop paying for something you're not using. Property taxes, insurance on the remaining structure, and basic upkeep continue whether or not you ever rebuild.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my house if my fire insurance claim isn't settled yet?

Yes. You can sell with an open claim. Depending on your policy and how far along the claim is, the remaining proceeds can sometimes be assigned to the buyer or credited at closing. A cash buyer familiar with this process can structure the sale around where your claim currently stands.

Is it better to rebuild or sell a fire-damaged lot in Sonoma County?

It depends on your insurance payout, your appetite for a multi-year rebuild, and current construction and permitting costs. Many owners who rebuilt after the Tubbs and Glass fires found the process took two to four years and cost more than their payout covered. Selling the land, or the land plus a partial structure, is often the faster and less risky path if you don't want to take on that project yourself.

Do I need to clear debris before selling a fire-damaged property?

No. A cash buyer will typically purchase the property with fire debris, a damaged foundation, or a partially standing structure still in place. You don't need to pay for a debris removal permit or hire a contractor before selling.

Does a fire-damaged property still have value if the house is a total loss?

Yes. The land itself retains value, and in many cases existing utility connections, an approved septic or sewer lateral, and any usable foundation add to what a buyer will pay compared to a truly vacant lot. Location and lot size still drive most of the value.

How long does it typically take to rebuild after a wildfire in Sonoma County?

Timelines varied significantly after the Tubbs and Glass fires, with many owners reporting two to four years from permit application to move-in, driven by contractor availability, permitting backlogs, and rising material costs. Some rebuilds took longer, particularly for custom or larger homes.
